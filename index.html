    How to read this book

    Prologue

    Every large language model does one thing at its core. It reads a sequence of tokens and assigns a probability to every possible next token. Everything else in this book, from attention to alignment, is a better way to do that one thing, or a way to make that one thing useful.

    The chapters follow the historical record. Each one introduces the papers that moved the field, explains the idea in plain terms, and gives you a laboratory: a small simulation that runs entirely in your browser. The simulations are toy models. They use real formulas from the cited papers, but at a scale you can see and change. Where a lab uses a published equation with fitted constants, the constants are the published ones.

    Sources were chosen from peer-reviewed venues: journals such as Nature, Science, JMLR and Neural Computation, and the main refereed conferences (NeurIPS, ICLR, ACL, NAACL, FAccT). A few influential documents never went through peer review, such as the GPT-1 and GPT-2 technical reports and the Kaplan scaling-law preprint. They are cited because the story cannot be told without them, and they are marked Preprint or Tech report in the bibliography.

    Part I · Foundations

    Counting the next letter

    Chapter 1 · 1948

    The idea of a language model is older than the computer that runs it. In 1948 Claude Shannon modelled English as a stochastic process, a source that emits symbols one at a time with probabilities that depend on what came before . He built a series of approximations by hand. Letters drawn at random produced gibberish. Letters drawn with their true frequencies looked a little better. Letters drawn according to the previous one or two letters began to produce fragments that resembled English words. Words drawn according to the previous word produced phrases that were locally plausible and globally meaningless.

    Shannon's interest was communication. A source that is predictable carries less information per symbol and can be compressed further. Prediction, compression and information are the same quantity seen from three sides. That equivalence still defines how language models are trained: the training loss of a modern model is its average surprise, in bits or nats, at the next token.

    For fifty years the working version of this idea was the n-gram model: count how often each sequence of n symbols appears, and predict from the counts. It powered speech recognition and statistical machine translation. Its weakness is data sparsity. The number of possible contexts grows exponentially with n, so most long contexts are never seen in training, and the model has nothing to say about them.

    Laboratory 1

    Shannon's approximations

    A character model is trained on a short paragraph about rivers and language. Set how many previous characters it may look at, then generate. At order 0 it samples letters by frequency alone. Around order 4 or 5 it starts copying whole phrases from its tiny corpus, which is the sparsity problem in miniature.

    Words become vectors

    Chapter 2 · 2003–2017

    Bengio and colleagues attacked sparsity directly . Their neural probabilistic language model learned two things at once: a vector of real numbers for every word, and a neural network that predicts the next word from the vectors of the previous few. Because similar words end up with similar vectors, a sentence the model has never seen can still receive a sensible probability if it resembles sentences it has seen. "The cat is walking in the bedroom" lends statistical strength to "a dog was running in a room."

    A decade later Mikolov and colleagues made word vectors cheap to train on billions of words with the skip-gram model and negative sampling . The vectors showed surprising linear structure: the offset from man to woman is close to the offset from king to queen, and the offset from a country to its capital is roughly constant.

    That geometry has a darker side. Vectors learned from human text absorb the associations in that text, including stereotypes about gender and race. Bolukbasi and colleagues found occupational gender analogies in widely used embeddings , and Caliskan and colleagues showed in Science that embeddings reproduce the implicit biases measured in human psychology experiments . Every model in the rest of this book inherits this property.

    Memory and its leaks

    Chapter 3 · 1986–2014

    A fixed window of previous words is a crude memory. Recurrent neural networks replaced it with a hidden state that is updated at every step and, in principle, carries information from the start of a sequence to its end. They are trained with backpropagation unrolled through time, and Elman showed that even simple recurrent networks discover grammatical structure such as word categories from sequences alone .

    In practice the memory leaked. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi proved that learning long-range dependencies with gradient descent is hard: the error signal is multiplied by a similar factor at every step, so over long spans it either vanishes or explodes . Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's Long Short-Term Memory answered with a memory cell whose content is copied forward by default and changed only through learned gates . A later addition, the forget gate, let the cell decide when to clear itself .

    LSTMs carried the field into the 2010s. Sutskever, Vinyals and Le used one LSTM to read a sentence into a single vector and another to write its translation . That single vector was the next bottleneck: a whole sentence, of any length, squeezed through a fixed-size pipe.

    Part II · The Transformer

    Attention

    Chapter 4 · 2015–2017

    Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio removed the bottleneck by letting the decoder look back . At each output word the model computes a score for every input word, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and reads a weighted average of the input. The weights form a soft alignment between the two sentences, learned without anyone labelling which word translates which.

    Vaswani and colleagues then asked what happens if attention is the only mechanism . The Transformer drops recurrence. Every token produces a query, a key and a value. Each token's new representation is a weighted average of all values, weighted by how well its query matches each key:

    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dk ) V

    Several such attention "heads" run in parallel, each free to learn a different relation. Because no step waits for the previous one, the whole sequence is processed at once on parallel hardware. The original model set a new state of the art on English–German and English–French translation at a fraction of the training cost of earlier systems. Within two years it had displaced recurrent networks almost everywhere in language processing.

    Cutting text into tokens

    Chapter 5 · 2016–2023

    A model with a fixed vocabulary of whole words cannot read a word it has never seen: a new name, a misspelling, a compound, a word from another language. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch borrowed byte pair encoding, a compression trick, to fix this for machine translation . Start from single characters. Repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair in the training data and merge it into a new symbol. After a few tens of thousands of merges, common words are single tokens and rare words split into familiar pieces.

    Almost every modern model uses a descendant of this scheme, so the "next token" is usually a word piece, not a word. This has consequences that are easy to miss. A tokenizer trained mostly on English text compresses English well and other scripts poorly. Petrov and colleagues measured the same content in many languages and found that some need several times more tokens than English . Since usage is priced and limited by tokens, speakers of those languages pay more and fit less into a model's context window. Bengali is among the languages that are expensive under English-centred tokenizers.

    Three recipes for pretraining

    Chapter 6 · 2018–2020

    Between 2018 and 2020 three ways of training Transformers on raw text competed. The difference between them is mostly which tokens may attend to which.

    Decoder only. OpenAI's GPT trained a Transformer to predict the next token, with each position allowed to see only the positions before it . Encoder only. Google's BERT hid 15 percent of input tokens and trained the model to recover them using context on both sides . Bidirectional context made BERT the strongest model for understanding tasks such as classification and question answering, and it topped the benchmarks of its day. Encoder–decoder. T5 cast every task, from translation to classification, as text in and text out, and ran a systematic comparison of objectives and architectures .

    The decoder-only design won the generative era. Brown and colleagues scaled it to 175 billion parameters in GPT-3 and showed that a model trained only to continue text could perform new tasks from a few examples written into the prompt, with no further training . They called this in-context learning. A single model that adapts through its prompt changed how language models are built and used.

    Part III · Scale

    The arithmetic of scale

    Chapter 7 · 2020–2022

    Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI reported that a language model's loss falls as a smooth power law in three quantities: the number of parameters N, the number of training tokens D, and the compute used C, across seven orders of magnitude . Their analysis suggested that, given more compute, most of it should go into a larger model. The paper was never peer reviewed, but it shaped how the largest models of 2020 and 2021 were trained.

    Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind revisited the question with more than 400 trained models and reached a different answer . Parameters and tokens should grow in equal proportion, roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. By that rule, the large models of the time were undertrained. Their 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens with the same compute as the 280-billion-parameter Gopher, outperformed it. One of their fitted forms is:

    L(N, D) = 1.69 + 406.4 / N0.34 + 410.7 / D0.28    with   C ≈ 6ND

    The constant 1.69 is the irreducible loss, the entropy of natural text that no model can remove. The other two terms are what a finite model and finite data leave on the table. In practice, developers now often train smaller models on far more than 20 tokens per parameter, because a smaller model is cheaper to run for millions of users. Chinchilla answers a narrower question: how to get the lowest loss for a fixed training budget.

    Choosing the next word

    Chapter 8 · 2018–2020

    A trained model gives a probability distribution. Turning that distribution into text is a separate decision, and it matters more than it seems. Always taking the most probable token, or searching for the most probable whole sequence with beam search, sounds ideal. Holtzman and colleagues showed that it produces bland, repetitive text that loops on itself, a problem they called neural text degeneration . Human writing is not the most probable text; it regularly contains words a model rates as unlikely.

    Pure random sampling fails the other way. The long tail of unlikely tokens is collectively large, and sampling from it now and then derails the text. Three controls are used together. Temperature rescales the logits before the softmax: below 1 sharpens the distribution, above 1 flattens it. Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens . Top-p, or nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, so the cut adapts to how confident the model is .

    Does ability emerge?

    Chapter 9 · 2022–2023

    Wei and colleagues catalogued tasks on which small models perform at chance and larger models suddenly do well, such as multi-digit arithmetic and some reasoning benchmarks . They called these abilities emergent: not present in smaller models and not predictable by extrapolating from them. The finding fed a worry that capabilities, including dangerous ones, might appear without warning.

    Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo offered a different explanation . Many emergent results use all-or-nothing metrics such as exact match. If a model must get every one of L tokens right, and its per-token accuracy p improves smoothly with scale, then the task score is pL, which sits near zero and then shoots up. Measured with a continuous metric, the same models improve gradually. The paper received an outstanding paper award at NeurIPS 2023.

    The debate is not fully closed. Smooth underlying improvement does not mean a capability is unimportant when it crosses a usable threshold, and a user who needs a correct answer experiences the exact-match curve. The lesson for evaluation is practical: what looks like a sudden leap may be an artefact of how success is scored, so report more than one metric.

    Part IV · Making models useful

    Teaching models what people want

    Chapter 10 · 2017–2023

    A pretrained model imitates its training text, which includes helpful answers, wrong answers and abuse. Making it follow instructions requires a signal about what people actually prefer. Christiano and colleagues showed that agents can learn complex behaviour from human comparisons of pairs of outcomes, by first fitting a reward model to the comparisons . The reward model uses the Bradley–Terry form: the probability that response A is preferred to B is σ(rA − rB).

    Ouyang and colleagues applied this to GPT-3 in three stages: supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, a reward model trained on human rankings, and reinforcement learning against that reward with a penalty for drifting too far from the starting model . Human raters preferred the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT to the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. Alignment with intent, not size, decided usefulness.

    Rafailov and colleagues noticed that this penalised objective has a closed-form optimum :

    π*(y | x) ∝ πref(y | x) · exp( r(x, y) / β )

    The new policy reweights the old one by exponentiated reward, and β sets how far it may move. Their method, Direct Preference Optimization, uses this relation to train on preference pairs directly, without a separate reward model or reinforcement learning loop. Both approaches share a limit: the model learns what raters reward, which is not always what is true.

    Thinking out loud

    Chapter 11 · 2022–2025

    A model that must produce an answer in one token has one forward pass to compute it. Wei and colleagues showed that prompting large models with examples that include intermediate reasoning steps, a chain of thought, sharply improves performance on arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning . The benefit appeared mainly in large models. Writing the steps gives the model more computation and a place to keep intermediate results.

    Wang and colleagues added self-consistency: sample many reasoning chains and take a majority vote over their final answers . Correct chains tend to converge on the same answer, while errors scatter across many different wrong answers, so the vote filters noise.

    The next step was to train the reasoning, not just prompt it. DeepSeek-AI reported in Nature that reinforcement learning with rewards based only on whether final answers were correct, on problems where correctness can be checked automatically, led a model to produce longer reasoning with behaviours such as self-verification and revisiting earlier steps . Reasoning models of this kind trade more computation at answer time for accuracy.

    Laboratory 11

    Looking things up

    Chapter 12 · 2020–2023

    A model's knowledge is frozen into its weights at training time, and when it lacks a fact it often produces a fluent, confident fabrication. Ji and colleagues' survey in ACM Computing Surveys documents this "hallucination" across summarisation, dialogue, question answering and translation .

    Lewis and colleagues proposed retrieval-augmented generation: before answering, retrieve relevant passages from a document collection and condition the answer on them . Knowledge then lives partly outside the model, where it can be updated, inspected and cited. Two further lines of work let models act. Toolformer taught a model to decide for itself when to call tools such as a calculator or search engine , and ReAct interleaved reasoning steps with actions and observations, so the model can search, read and revise its plan . These loops are the basis of today's AI agents.

    Retrieval reduces fabrication but does not remove it. A model can misread a correct passage, and the answer is only as good as what is retrieved. For public institutions, this is the main argument for grounding systems in an authoritative document store and showing citations to the reader.

    Doing more with less

    Chapter 13 · 2017–2023

    Scale is expensive, and much recent work makes it cheaper. A mixture of experts replaces one large feed-forward block with many smaller "experts" and a router that sends each token to only a few of them . Total parameters can grow while the computation per token stays nearly flat. Switch Transformers simplified routing to a single expert per token and trained models with over a trillion parameters .

    Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) makes fine-tuning cheap . Instead of updating a large weight matrix W, it freezes W and learns a correction as the product of two thin matrices, W + BA, where B and A have a small rank r. For GPT-3 the authors reported about 10,000 times fewer trainable parameters than full fine-tuning, with comparable quality. QLoRA combined this with 4-bit quantisation of the frozen model, so a 65-billion-parameter model could be fine-tuned on a single 48 GB GPU . FlashAttention reorganised the attention computation around GPU memory to make it several times faster without approximation .

    These methods matter for any institution that wants to adapt open models to its own language and documents on modest hardware.

    Epilogue

    What the record does not settle

    Epilogue

    Seventy-five years after Shannon, the core task is unchanged: predict the next token. What changed is the representation (vectors instead of counts), the mechanism (attention instead of recurrence), the scale (trillions of tokens), and the training signal (human and verifiable feedback layered on top of prediction).

    Several questions remain open in the peer-reviewed literature. Bender and colleagues argued that ever-larger models trained on uncurated web text carry environmental and financial costs, encode the biases of their data, and produce fluent text that readers may mistake for understanding . Weidinger and colleagues set out a taxonomy of risks from language models, from discrimination and misinformation to malicious use and environmental harm . Strubell, Ganesh and McCallum quantified the energy and carbon cost of training NLP models . Hallucination remains an active research problem .

    For a government reader, three practical lessons follow from the chapters above. Evaluate models on your own language and documents, because tokenization and training data make performance uneven across languages (Chapter 5). Ground answers in authoritative sources the reader can check (Chapter 12). Report evaluation results with more than one metric and keep a human accountable for decisions (Chapters 9 and 10).
